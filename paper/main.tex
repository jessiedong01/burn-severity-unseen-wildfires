\begin{abstract}
Burn severity maps determine where post-fire erosion control, debris-flow warnings, and reforestation are deployed.
In the United States, the Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity (MTBS) program produces these maps by thresholding the differenced Normalized Burn Ratio (dNBR), with thresholds chosen by an analyst for each fire.
Automatic methods are evaluated here on how closely they reproduce these maps on fires excluded from training.
The dataset contains \mFires{} California and Oregon wildfires from \mYearMin{} to \mYearMax{} (\mAcresM{} million acres, \mPixelsM{} million labeled pixels) on the exact Landsat scenes MTBS used.
Five-fold cross-validation holds out whole fires together with the fires that overlap them.
The methods are dNBR with generic thresholds, dNBR, RdNBR, and RBR with thresholds learned on the training fires, a per-pixel gradient-boosting classifier, and two U-Net variants.
Learned thresholds raise mean mIoU from \mdnbrgenericMiou{} to \mdnbrlearnedMiou{} and improve \mWinsLearnGen{} of \mFires{} fires.
Neither U-Net improves on calibrated dNBR.
The better variant reaches \mBestUnetMiou, a paired difference of \mDiffLearned{} (95\% CI \mDiffLearnedLo{} to \mDiffLearnedHi).
Each fire's own analyst thresholds reach \moracleMiou, and their advantage on a fire grows with the distance between its analyst thresholds and the learned ones (Spearman $\rho=\aGapRho$).
Of the remaining errors, \aDnbrLearnedAdjShare\% confuse adjacent classes and \aDnbrLearnedErrNearShare\% lie within 30\,m of a class transition in the MTBS map.
The remaining error is therefore fire-specific calibration rather than missing spectral or spatial information.
An earlier version of the evaluation, which derived labels from dNBR and tested on training fires, overstated U-Net accuracy, and the sources of that error are quantified.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

After a large wildfire, land managers need a map of how severely the fire altered vegetation and soil.
The map guides where crews place erosion control, which watersheds face elevated debris-flow risk in the first rains, and which stands require replanting rather than natural regeneration.
In the United States, the Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity (MTBS) program produces these maps for every fire larger than 1{,}000 acres in the West \citep{eidenshink2007mtbs}.
An MTBS analyst selects a pre-fire and a post-fire Landsat scene, computes the differenced Normalized Burn Ratio (dNBR) \citep{key2006landscape}, and chooses dNBR thresholds that separate unburned, low, moderate, and high severity for that fire.
For many forested fires, MTBS uses a post-fire scene from the next growing season, which it calls an extended assessment.
The resulting maps are the reference product for burn severity in the United States, but each requires an analyst's judgment.

The analyst's thresholds differ from fire to fire.
Across the \mFires{} fires in the dataset, the threshold between moderate and high severity ranges from \mHighMin{} to \mHighMax{} dNBR units, and every value exceeds the generic threshold of 440 that is often used in its place (\cref{fig:thresholds}).
A method that reproduces MTBS maps automatically must therefore either estimate a fire's thresholds from its imagery or learn a mapping from imagery to severity that does not depend on them.
Deep segmentation models take the second approach.
They use all six reflective bands rather than one index, and they use spatial context.

Three choices make the comparison between these approaches informative.
First, every method is scored against MTBS analyst maps rather than against labels derived from the same imagery.
Labels computed from dNBR with fixed thresholds are a closed-form function of the model's inputs, and a model trained on them can at best recover that function.
Second, the inputs are the exact Landsat scenes named in each fire's MTBS record, warped onto the 30\,m grid of the MTBS mosaic, so labels are never resampled and inputs match what the analyst saw.
Third, whole fires are held out.
Fires that overlap spatially share a group, and groups are assigned to five cross-validation folds, so every fire is predicted by a model that has seen neither that fire nor any fire that overlaps it.

Under these conditions, threshold calibration dominates.
Replacing the generic dNBR thresholds with thresholds learned on the training fires raises mean mIoU by \mDiffLearnGen{} (95\% CI \mDiffLearnGenLo{} to \mDiffLearnGenHi).
Two U-Net variants, one on the twelve reflectance bands and one with five additional index channels, match calibrated dNBR but do not exceed it, with paired differences of \mDiffBandsLearned{} and \mDiffLearned{} and intervals that include zero.
An oracle that applies each test fire's own analyst thresholds reaches \moracleMiou, and its advantage on a fire increases with the distance between that fire's analyst thresholds and the learned ones.
Nearly all remaining errors confuse adjacent severity classes, and they concentrate at transitions between classes in the MTBS map.
\Cref{sec:revisit} shows how label construction and evaluation leakage inflated the results of an earlier version of the evaluation.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

For a fire $f$, let $\mathbf{x}^{\text{pre}}_f, \mathbf{x}^{\text{post}}_f \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times H \times W}$ be Landsat surface reflectance in bands 2--7 (blue, green, red, near infrared, shortwave infrared 1 and 2) before and after the fire, and let $\Omega_f$ be the set of pixels inside the MTBS fire perimeter that are not masked.
The label $y_f(i) \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ for $i \in \Omega_f$ is the MTBS severity class (unburned, low, moderate, high).
The Normalized Burn Ratio of an image is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{NBR} = \frac{\rho_{\text{NIR}} - \rho_{\text{SWIR2}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}} + \rho_{\text{SWIR2}}},
\end{equation}
and $\mathrm{dNBR}_f = 1000\,(\mathrm{NBR}^{\text{pre}}_f - \mathrm{NBR}^{\text{post}}_f)$.
MTBS produces labels approximately as
\begin{equation}
  y_f(i) \approx q\big(\mathrm{dNBR}_f(i);\, \boldsymbol{\tau}_f\big), \qquad q(v; \boldsymbol{\tau}) = \sum_{k=1}^{3} \mathbb{1}[v \geq \tau_k],
  \label{eq:mtbs}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\tau}_f = (\tau_{f,1}, \tau_{f,2}, \tau_{f,3})$ are thresholds chosen by the analyst for fire $f$.
The approximation reflects manual edits and differences between the reflectance processing used here and that of MTBS.

Given a set of training fires $\mathcal{F}_{\text{train}}$ with labels, the task is to predict $y_{f^*}$ for a fire $f^* \notin \mathcal{F}_{\text{train}}$ from $\mathbf{x}^{\text{pre}}_{f^*}$ and $\mathbf{x}^{\text{post}}_{f^*}$ alone.
\Cref{eq:mtbs} suggests two strategies.
One estimates thresholds that transfer across fires.
The other learns a mapping from reflectance to severity that does not depend on $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f^*}$.
A predictor that knew $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f^*}$ would reproduce the analyst's rule up to manual edits, and it serves as an oracle.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Fire Selection}
The dataset contains every wildfire in the MTBS perimeter database that (i) started in California or Oregon in 2018, 2020, or 2021, (ii) burned at least 60{,}000 acres, (iii) was mapped with an extended assessment, in which the post-fire scene is acquired in the growing season after the fire, and (iv) used Landsat 8 or 9 for both scenes.
These criteria yield \mFires{} fires (\cref{tab:fires} in \cref{app:fires}) covering \mAcresM{} million acres, from Sierra Nevada mixed conifer to the Klamath Mountains, the western Oregon Cascades, the Coast Ranges, and southern California chaparral.
The number of labeled pixels per fire ranges from \aPxMin{} to \aPxMax{} million.

\subsection{Imagery}
The pre- and post-fire scenes identified in each MTBS record are read from the Landsat Collection~2 Level-2 archive \citep{usgs2026landsatc2} hosted on Microsoft Planetary Computer \citep{planetarycomputer}.
For \mSubstituted{} fires the archive does not contain the exact post-fire scene, and the closest Landsat 9 scene of the same path and row, acquired 8 days later, replaces it.
Digital numbers are converted to surface reflectance with the Collection~2 scale factor and offset ($\rho = 2.75 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{DN} - 0.2$).
Each band is warped with bilinear resampling onto the 30\,m Albers grid of the MTBS severity mosaic, which leaves the labels on their native grid.
Pixels that the Level-2 quality band flags as fill, cloud, dilated cloud, cirrus, cloud shadow, or snow in either scene are masked.
To check alignment, each fire's analyst thresholds are applied to the dNBR computed here and compared with the MTBS map under one-pixel shifts.
For \aAlignShifted{} of \mFires{} fires (\aAlignShiftedEighteen{} of the \aAlignNEighteen{} fires from 2018 and \aAlignShiftedTwentyOne{} from 2021), agreement is highest at a one-pixel shift, by \aAlignGainMin{} to \aAlignGainMax{} percentage points.
The offset is not corrected, and it affects every method equally.

\subsection{Labels}
Labels come from the MTBS thematic burn severity mosaics for 2018, 2020, and 2021 \citep{mtbs2024mosaics}, restricted to each fire's perimeter so that neighboring fires in the mosaic are excluded.
The six MTBS classes are unburned to low, low, moderate, high, increased greenness, and a non-processing mask.
Unburned to low and increased greenness map to \emph{unburned}, low, moderate, and high are kept, and the non-processing mask is ignored.
The \mPixelsM{} million labeled pixels are \mShareUnburned\% unburned, \mShareLow\% low, \mShareModerate\% moderate, and \mShareHigh\% high.
The share of high severity varies from \aHighShareMin\% to \aHighShareMax\% across fires.

\subsection{Analyst Thresholds}
Each MTBS record stores the analyst's three dNBR thresholds.
Across the \mFires{} fires the unburned/low threshold ranges from \mLowMin{} to \mLowMax, the low/moderate threshold from \mModMin{} to \mModMax, and the moderate/high threshold from \mHighMin{} to \mHighMax{} (\cref{fig:thresholds}).
The generic values of 100, 270, and 440 follow the class edges of \citet{key2006landscape}.
In their scheme, 440--660 is a moderate-high class and values above 660 are high.

\subsection{Cross-Validation by Fire}
Three pairs of fires overlap.
The 2020 North Complex burned into the 2018 Camp Fire scar, the 2021 McFarland Fire into the 2020 August Complex, and the 2021 Bootleg Fire into the 2018 Watson Creek Fire.
Fires that share more than 1\% of the smaller fire's area are placed in the same group, and a greedy procedure assigns groups to five folds while balancing burned area.
Each fold contains \aFoldFiresMin{} to \aFoldFiresMax{} fires and \aFoldAcresMin{} to \aFoldAcresMax{} million acres.
Every method is trained on four folds and evaluated on the fifth.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{Spectral indices.}
Two relativized indices complement dNBR, the relativized dNBR \citep{miller2007rdnbr} and the relativized burn ratio \citep{parks2014rbr}, both scaled by 1000,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RdNBR} = \frac{\mathrm{dNBR}}{\sqrt{|\mathrm{NBR}^{\text{pre}}|}}, \qquad
  \mathrm{RBR} = \frac{\mathrm{dNBR}}{\mathrm{NBR}^{\text{pre}} + 1.001}.
\end{equation}
Both normalize dNBR by pre-fire vegetation, which reduces the dependence of dNBR on pre-fire biomass.

\paragraph{Index thresholding.}
Each threshold classifier applies $q(\cdot; \boldsymbol{\tau})$ from \cref{eq:mtbs} to one index.
The generic classifier applies dNBR with $\boldsymbol{\tau} = (100, 270, 440)$.
The learned classifier uses thresholds that maximize the mean mIoU over training fires,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\tau}} \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}_{\text{train}}|} \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}_{\text{train}}} \mathrm{mIoU}_f\big(q(\mathrm{index}_f; \boldsymbol{\tau})\big),
\end{equation}
found by coordinate search over integer thresholds with step sizes 64, 16, 4, and 1, using per-fire histograms of the index by class.
Weighting fires equally prevents the largest fires from determining the thresholds.
The oracle applies dNBR with the test fire's own analyst thresholds $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f^*}$.
It uses information from the test fire and serves as a reference ceiling, not a baseline.

\paragraph{Gaussian smoothing and multi-Otsu.}
This pipeline comes from the earlier version of the evaluation.
A Gaussian filter ($\sigma = 1.5$ pixels) smooths dNBR, and four-class multi-level Otsu thresholding \citep{otsu1979threshold} splits the smoothed values inside the perimeter of each fire.
It requires no training.

\paragraph{Per-pixel gradient boosting.}
A histogram-based gradient-boosting classifier \citep{ke2017lightgbm, pedregosa2011sklearn} uses 300 iterations, 63 leaves per tree, and class weights inversely proportional to class frequency.
Each pixel has 17 features, the 12 reflectance values together with $\mathrm{NBR}^{\text{pre}}$, $\mathrm{NBR}^{\text{post}}$, dNBR, RdNBR, and RBR.
Training uses 60{,}000 labeled pixels sampled uniformly from each training fire.
The classifier has no spatial context.

\paragraph{U-Net.}
The U-Net \citep{ronneberger2015unet} has four encoder stages of 32, 64, 128, and 256 channels, a 512-channel bottleneck, transposed-convolution upsampling, and skip connections (7.8M parameters).
Two variants differ in their inputs.
One uses the 12 reflectance bands (\emph{12 bands}).
The other adds the five index channels (\emph{12 bands + indices}), with dNBR, RdNBR, and RBR divided by 1000 and RdNBR clipped to $[-5, 5]$.
Each channel is standardized with the mean and standard deviation of valid pixels in the training fires.
Masked pixels are set to zero after standardization.
Training samples $256 \times 256$ patches centered on labeled pixels, selects a fire with probability proportional to the square root of its number of labeled pixels, and applies random horizontal and vertical flips and $90^\circ$ rotations.
The loss is cross-entropy over labeled pixels, with class weights proportional to the inverse square root of class frequency in the training fires.
Training runs for 3{,}000 steps with batch size 16, using AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} with weight decay $10^{-4}$ and a one-cycle learning-rate schedule \citep{smith2019superconvergence} that peaks at $10^{-3}$.
The number of steps is fixed in advance, and no checkpoint is selected on held-out data.
At test time a $256 \times 256$ sliding window at stride 128 covers each fire.
A two-dimensional Hann window weights overlapping predictions, and reflection padding ensures that every pixel is predicted.
\Cref{app:impl} lists all hyperparameters.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Metrics.}
For a fire $f$ and class $c$, $\mathrm{IoU}_{f,c} = \mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{FN})$ over the labeled pixels $\Omega_f$.
The fire's mIoU averages $\mathrm{IoU}_{f,c}$ over the classes present in the label or the prediction.
Macro-F1 and pixel accuracy are also reported.
Two aggregates appear in the results.
The mean over fires weights every fire equally, and the pooled mIoU of the summed confusion matrices weights every pixel equally.
The two differ because the number of labeled pixels per fire differs by a factor of \aPxRatio.

\paragraph{Statistical comparison.}
Two methods $A$ and $B$ are compared by the mean of the per-fire difference $\mathrm{mIoU}_f(A) - \mathrm{mIoU}_f(B)$, with a 95\% confidence interval from 10{,}000 bootstrap resamples of fires \citep{efron1993bootstrap}.
Resampling fires rather than pixels treats the fire as the unit of generalization.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
All experiments run on one Apple M5 Pro laptop with 64\,GB of memory.
Index thresholding and gradient boosting run in minutes on the CPU.
Each U-Net fold trains in about \aMinPerFold{} minutes on the integrated GPU (\aSecPerStep{} seconds per step) with PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch}.

\paragraph{Learning the thresholds accounts for most of the improvement over generic dNBR.}
Thresholds learned on the training fires raise mean mIoU from \mdnbrgenericMiou{} to \mdnbrlearnedMiou{} (paired difference \mDiffLearnGen, 95\% CI \mDiffLearnGenLo{} to \mDiffLearnGenHi) and improve \mWinsLearnGen{} of \mFires{} fires.
The learned thresholds are \mLearnedLowMin--\mLearnedLowMax, \mLearnedModMin--\mLearnedModMax, and \mLearnedHighMin--\mLearnedHighMax{} across folds (\cref{tab:learned}).
They lie close to the analysts' typical values.
The low/moderate and moderate/high thresholds are well above the generic 270 and 440.
The largest per-class change is for moderate severity, whose IoU rises from \mdnbrgenericIouModerate{} to \mdnbrlearnedIouModerate.

\paragraph{Neither U-Net improves on calibrated dNBR.}
The U-Net on 12 bands reaches \munetBandsMiou{} and the U-Net with index channels reaches \munetIdxMiou, against \mdnbrlearnedMiou{} for dNBR with learned thresholds.
The paired differences are \mDiffBandsLearned{} (95\% CI \mDiffBandsLearnedLo{} to \mDiffBandsLearnedHi) and \mDiffLearned{} (\mDiffLearnedLo{} to \mDiffLearnedHi), and the U-Nets are better on \mWinsBandsLearned{} and \mWinsLearned{} of \mFires{} fires.
Adding index channels to the U-Net changes mIoU by \mDiffIdxBands{} (\mDiffIdxBandsLo{} to \mDiffIdxBandsHi).
The U-Net with index channels is slightly better than per-pixel gradient boosting (\mDiffGbm, 95\% CI \mDiffGbmLo{} to \mDiffGbmHi), which indicates that spatial context improves on a classifier without it, but not on calibrated dNBR.

\paragraph{Relativized indices do not improve on dNBR.}
With learned thresholds, RBR reaches \mrbrlearnedMiou{} and RdNBR \mrdnbrlearnedMiou.
RdNBR divides by the square root of pre-fire NBR, which becomes unstable where pre-fire NBR is near zero.
Its standard deviation across fires (\mrdnbrlearnedMiouSd) is twice that of dNBR (\mdnbrlearnedMiouSd).
Because MTBS labels are defined by dNBR thresholds (\cref{eq:mtbs}), an index other than dNBR is at a disadvantage against these labels regardless of how well it measures field severity.

\paragraph{Multi-Otsu thresholding performs worst.}
The Gaussian and multi-Otsu pipeline reaches \motsuMiou.
Otsu's method places thresholds that maximize the between-class variance of each fire's smoothed dNBR histogram, and these need not coincide with severity classes.
Its standard deviation across fires (\motsuMiouSd) is the largest of all methods.

\paragraph{The remaining error is fire-specific calibration.}
The oracle reaches \moracleMiou, \mDiffOracle{} above learned dNBR (95\% CI \mDiffOracleLo{} to \mDiffOracleHi), and is better on \mWinsOracle{} fires.
For each fire, the distance between its analyst thresholds and the learned thresholds of its fold is $\sum_k |\tau_{f,k} - \hat{\tau}_k|$.
The oracle's advantage over learned dNBR increases with this distance (Spearman $\rho = \aGapRho$, $p \aGapP$, \cref{fig:calibration}).
The fires on which learned thresholds fall furthest short are the fires whose analysts departed most from typical thresholds.

\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Generic thresholds over-predict severity.}
Of the errors made by generic dNBR, \aDnbrGenericOverShare\% assign a higher class than MTBS.
High severity has \aDnbrGenericRecHigh\% recall but \aDnbrGenericPrecHigh\% precision, and moderate severity has \aDnbrGenericRecModerate\% recall, because the generic moderate/high threshold of 440 is below every analyst's choice (\cref{tab:perclass}).

\paragraph{Errors confuse adjacent classes.}
For each of the four methods in \cref{fig:confusion}, at least \aDnbrGenericAdjShare\% of errors confuse classes that are adjacent in the severity order.
No method confuses unburned with high severity at a measurable rate.
The task is therefore one of placing boundaries between neighboring classes, which is the role of thresholds in \cref{eq:mtbs}.

\paragraph{Errors concentrate at class transitions.}
Each labeled pixel is assigned its distance to the nearest labeled pixel of a different MTBS class.
MTBS maps are spatially fragmented, and \aBinShareA\% of labeled pixels lie within 30\,m of another class.
The error rate of learned dNBR is \aDnbrLearnedErrNear\% within 30\,m of a transition and \aDnbrLearnedErrFar\% beyond 240\,m, and \aDnbrLearnedErrNearShare\% of its errors lie within 30\,m of a transition (\cref{fig:boundary}).

\paragraph{A single high threshold of about \aSweepBest{} fits these fires.}
With the unburned/low and low/moderate thresholds fixed at \aSweepLow{} and \aSweepMod, mean mIoU over all fires peaks at \aSweepBestMiou{} for a moderate/high threshold of \aSweepBest{} and falls to \aSweepAtGeneric{} at 440 (\cref{fig:calibration}, right).
Mean mIoU stays within 0.01 of its maximum for thresholds between \aSweepFlatLo{} and \aSweepFlatHi, and the generic threshold lies well outside this range.

\paragraph{Training stability.}
In the first run of the 12-band U-Net, the training loss of one fold became undefined (NaN) at about step 2{,}600, and that fold's model predicted every pixel as unburned (mean mIoU \mBandsFoldFourBefore{} on its fires).
That fold alone was retrained with gradient-norm clipping at \mBandsClip, which gave \mBandsFoldFourAfter.
The other nine U-Net folds were trained without clipping and are reported unchanged.
The variant with index channels trained stably on every fold.

\section{Revisiting an Earlier Evaluation}
\label{sec:revisit}

An earlier version of the evaluation \citep{dong2026earlier} reported a mean mIoU of 0.756 for a U-Net and 0.357 for the Gaussian and multi-Otsu pipeline on five of these fires.
Its protocol differed from the present one in two respects, each of which inflates U-Net results.

\paragraph{Labels derived from the inputs.}
The earlier labels were dNBR thresholded at 100, 270, and 440, computed from the same imagery the U-Net received.
Because dNBR is a closed-form function of two of the twelve input bands, applying the label rule itself scores an mIoU of 1.0, and a U-Net only needs to approximate that rule.
The classical baseline received dNBR but replaced the label thresholds with Otsu thresholds, which guaranteed a lower score.
The comparison could not establish whether the reflectance bands carry information beyond dNBR.

For the Camp Fire, they agree with the MTBS map on \mCampOrigAgree\% of pixels inside the perimeter, label \mCampOrigHigh\% of the area as high severity where MTBS labels \mCampMtbsHigh\%, and assign \mCampOrigModAsHigh\% of MTBS moderate pixels to the high class.
\Cref{tab:revisit} separates the causes.
Generic thresholds applied to surface reflectance from the MTBS scenes agree with MTBS on \mCampGenericPhys\% of pixels.
A per-scene 2nd--98th percentile stretch of each band, as in the earlier pipeline, reduces agreement to \mCampStretch\%.
Post-fire composites from November and December 2018, acquired within weeks of the fire rather than in the next summer, reduce it to \mCampOrigScenes\% without the stretch.

\paragraph{Evaluation on training fires.}
The earlier U-Net was trained on 85\% of $256 \times 256$ patches drawn from all five fires and evaluated by predicting the same five fires in full.
Validation patches were a random 15\% of patches extracted at stride 128, so they overlapped training patches by up to half their area, and the learning curves could not reveal overfitting.

\paragraph{Other differences.}
Canny edges were computed but did not enter the prediction.
Cloud-masked pixels and image borders were filled with zeros, which yields $\mathrm{dNBR} = 0$ and the unburned label.
SIFT registration \citep{lowe2004sift} was applied to scenes that were already on a common grid.
A plotting error showed the ground-truth map in the panel labeled as the classical prediction in the per-fire comparison figures.

The present protocol removes each of these issues.
Labels come from MTBS, inputs are the MTBS scenes in surface reflectance, masked pixels are excluded from training and scoring, and whole fires are held out.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Spectral indices for burn severity.}
The Normalized Burn Ratio contrasts near-infrared reflectance, which healthy vegetation reflects strongly, with shortwave-infrared reflectance, which increases after fire.
\citet{key2006landscape} defined dNBR and a field protocol, the Composite Burn Index, for calibrating it.
\citet{miller2007rdnbr} proposed RdNBR to reduce the dependence of dNBR on pre-fire vegetation, and \citet{parks2014rbr} proposed RBR, which corresponded more closely to field measurements across 18 fires than either dNBR or RdNBR.
MTBS \citep{eidenshink2007mtbs} applies analyst-selected dNBR thresholds fire by fire, which makes its maps both the standard reference and a function of per-fire calibration.

\paragraph{Deep learning for burn severity.}
U-Net \citep{ronneberger2015unet} is widely used for dense prediction in remote sensing.
\citet{hu2023burnseverity} trained several U-Net variants on MTBS labels with Landsat imagery across the contiguous United States and found that an attention U-Net performed best among those tested.
The comparison here places a U-Net against calibrated index thresholds under an evaluation that holds out whole fires and their spatial neighbors, and it adds an oracle that isolates the effect of per-fire calibration.

\paragraph{Thresholding.}
Otsu's method \citep{otsu1979threshold} selects thresholds that maximize between-class variance of a histogram and requires no labels.
Learned thresholds require labeled fires but target the classes directly.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Implications.}
For reproducing MTBS maps on new fires, a dNBR classifier with thresholds learned from previous fires is as accurate as the U-Net evaluated here, requires no GPU, and has three interpretable parameters.
The remaining difference from the analyst is a per-fire calibration problem.
A model that predicts a fire's thresholds from its vegetation, terrain, and imagery targets that difference directly, and the oracle bounds what such a model could gain in this setting.

\paragraph{Labels are expert maps, not field truth.}
MTBS maps are derived from dNBR with analyst thresholds and manual edits.
They measure agreement with an analyst, not with field severity, and they favor dNBR over other indices by construction.
Evaluating against field measurements such as the Composite Burn Index would test burn severity itself.

\paragraph{Scope.}
The fires come from two states and three years, and fold assignment is fixed.
The evaluation covers one U-Net architecture with one training budget, and each fold is trained once.
Larger models, longer training, or pretraining on more regions may change the comparison.
For two fires the post-fire scene was acquired 8 days after the scene MTBS used, and for \aAlignShifted{} fires the imagery and the MTBS labels are offset by up to one pixel (\cref{sec:data}).
The surface-reflectance processing differs from MTBS processing, which contributes to the oracle's error.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Automatic burn severity mapping was evaluated against MTBS analyst maps on \mFires{} wildfires, with whole fires and their spatial neighbors held out.
dNBR with thresholds learned on other fires reaches a mean mIoU of \mdnbrlearnedMiou, and two U-Net variants reach the same accuracy.
An oracle with each fire's own analyst thresholds reaches \moracleMiou, and its advantage tracks how far a fire's thresholds depart from typical values.
Remaining errors confuse adjacent classes at class transitions.
Under this evaluation, the limiting factor for automatic burn severity mapping is fire-specific threshold calibration.
